# GenomeX vs CheckM2

CheckM2 estimates completeness and contamination with a machine-learning model over a reference proteome database. GenomeX uses BUSCO odb10 single-copy markers plus tetranucleotide composition. The two are not expected to produce identical numbers, and identical numbers would be suspicious. This page separates what must agree from what need not.

72 genomes compared, scanned against `bacteria_odb10` (124 markers). Generated by `bench/compare_to_checkm2.py`.

## What changed after the first run of this page, and what it bought

The first version of this comparison flagged 62 of 72 assemblies and its verdict
medians were flat -- 1.12% clean, 1.03% possible, 0.82% likely. The detector was
scoring a 3 kb contig and a 4 Mb chromosome against one threshold, and since a
tetranucleotide frequency is a multinomial sample whose divergence falls as
1/length, it was flagging the short tail of every fragmented assembly.

Composition is now scored against a null built from windows of the contig's own
length, tiled inside the assembly's own contigs. Development was done against
`bench/fragmentation_ladder.py` -- one genome shredded to 10, 50, 100, 300 and
1000 pieces, where the right answer is known from construction -- so that no
threshold was ever fitted to the numbers on this page.

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| genomes flagged of 72 | 62 | 13 |
| called clean | 10 | 59 |
| CheckM2 median by verdict | 1.12 / 1.03 / 0.82 (flat, faintly inverted) | 0.93 / 1.23 / 2.13 (monotone) |
| recall at CheckM2 >= 5% | 1.00 (4/4) | 0.25 (1/4) |
| precision at CheckM2 >= 5% | 0.06 | 0.08 |
| ladder rungs called clean | 2/15 | 13/15 |
| corr(contig score, log length) | -0.28 to -0.43 | -0.09 |

Two of those rows must be read together. The verdict now carries ordering
information it did not have before -- median CheckM2 contamination rises
monotonically across the three levels -- and the false-positive rate fell by a
factor of five. It cost recall: three genomes CheckM2 puts above 5% are now
called clean. Reporting the first without the second would be dishonest.

## Why the two tools disagree at 5%

The disagreement is not obviously GenomeX being wrong, and the evidence points at
a resolution limit rather than a defect.

**GenomeX's marker duplication is uncorrelated with CheckM2's contamination**
(r = -0.026 over 72 genomes). The genomes CheckM2 scores at 5.17%, 5.66% and
9.35% duplicate 0.81%, 0.00% and 0.81% of the BUSCO core respectively -- that is
zero or one marker out of 124. Those duplication figures match BUSCO 5.8.3 on
496 of 496 markers, so they are not a GenomeX artefact.

One marker is 0.81% of the core, so 124 markers quantise contamination in steps
of nearly a percent. Detecting 5% contamination through marker duplication
requires roughly six duplicated markers; observing zero or one is equally
consistent with a clean genome and with a contaminant that happens not to
duplicate those particular loci. CheckM2 works over KO annotations across
thousands of genes and is not subject to that granularity.

**The compositional signal was found, then explained away.** Each of the three
missed genomes carries 7, 7 and 18 candidate replicon groups. The composition
analysis located distinct sequence in every one of them; the marker test then
classified it as extrachromosomal, because it carries none of the single-copy
core -- which is the correct inference for a plasmid and the wrong one for a
foreign fragment that happens to carry no core genes.

That ambiguity is real and is not resolvable by adding thresholds. Separating a
plasmid from a low-abundance contaminant needs coverage depth, which this
pipeline does not have: every production binner uses composition and coverage
together, and composition alone cannot reach that decision.

## What this means for a user

Use the module to locate compositionally distinct sequence, which it now does
with fragmentation-invariant behaviour and a stated error rate. Read
`contigs.tsv`, the `replicon_candidate` groups, and the reasons.

Do not read the verdict as a MIMAG-style contamination percentage, and do not
substitute it for CheckM2 or CheckM if that number is what you need. A genome
called `clean` here can carry several percent of foreign sequence that neither
duplicates core markers nor forms a compositionally coherent block.
